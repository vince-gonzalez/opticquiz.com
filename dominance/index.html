About the dominant eye test

Just as you have a dominant hand, you have a dominant eye — the one your brain leans on to aim and judge direction. This test uses the Miles test, the standard quick procedure: you frame a distant object through a small triangle made with both hands, then close each eye in turn. Whichever eye keeps the object centered is your sighting-dominant eye. About two-thirds of people are right-eye dominant, and dominance doesn't have to match your handedness — "cross-dominance" is common.

Knowing yours is genuinely useful for anything you aim: put your dominant eye to the camera viewfinder, and in archery, rifle and pistol shooting, darts, or golf putting, set your stance to the dominant-eye side. It says nothing about eye health or which eye sees more sharply — it's about alignment, not acuity.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find my dominant eye?
Make a small triangle with both hands, frame a distant object with both eyes open, then close each eye. The eye that keeps it framed is dominant — that's the Miles test this page guides you through.
What does a dominant eye mean?
It's the eye your brain favors for aiming, like handedness. It doesn't indicate eye health or sharpness. Roughly two-thirds of people are right-eye dominant.
Why does eye dominance matter?
Mainly for aiming sports and photography — it tells you which eye to sight with and which side to set your stance.
Can your dominant eye change?
It's usually stable, though it can seem to shift with gaze direction, and some people have weak or mixed dominance. Unclear result? Retry with a smaller triangle and a farther target.
