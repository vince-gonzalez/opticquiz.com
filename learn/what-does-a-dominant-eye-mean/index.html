What Does a Dominant Eye Mean?

Your dominant eye is the one your brain relies on to aim and judge direction — similar to being right- or left-handed. About two-thirds of people are right-eye dominant. It says nothing about eye health or which eye sees more sharply; it simply anchors where things are. Knowing yours matters most for photography, archery, shooting, darts, and other aiming tasks.

What eye dominance actually is

When both eyes are open, your brain leans on one eye’s view to pin down direction and alignment — that’s your sighting-dominant eye. It’s a normal, built-in preference, not a strength or weakness. Roughly two in three people are right-eye dominant, the rest left, and a small minority show weak or mixed dominance.

Eye dominance and handedness usually match but don’t have to. Being right-handed and left-eye dominant is called cross-dominance, and it’s common.

Why it matters, and how to find yours

Anywhere you aim, your dominant eye should lead: put it to the camera viewfinder, and in archery, rifle and pistol shooting, darts, or golf putting, set your stance to the dominant-eye side. Cross-dominant shooters often learn to shoot from their eye-dominant side rather than their handed side.

The quickest way to find yours is the Miles triangle test: frame a distant object through a small triangle made with both hands, then close each eye — whichever eye keeps it framed is dominant.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know which eye is dominant?
Make a small triangle with both hands, center a distant object through it with both eyes open, then close each eye. The eye that keeps the object framed is your dominant eye.
Can you be right-handed and left-eye dominant?
Yes. That’s cross-dominance, and it’s common. Eye dominance and handedness often match but don’t have to.
Does your dominant eye ever change?
Sighting dominance is usually stable, though it can appear to shift with gaze direction in some people, and a minority have weak or mixed dominance.
Why does dominant eye matter for shooting?
You aim along your dominant eye’s line of sight. Matching your stance and sighting to the dominant eye keeps the target and sights aligned; cross-dominant shooters often adapt their stance.
