Am I Colorblind?

You can’t be certain without a test, but common signs of color blindness include confusing reds, greens and browns, seeing traffic-light reds as dim or muddy, struggling with color-coded charts, and not seeing numbers that others read in dot-pattern images. A quick Ishihara-style test flags a likely deficiency in about two minutes; an eye-care professional confirms it.

Everyday signs to watch for

Color blindness rarely feels like “missing” color — more often it’s specific confusions you’ve lived with so long they seem normal. The most common signs of red-green deficiency are:

Confusing reds with greens and browns, especially in dim light; traffic-light reds looking dim or grayish; trouble with color-coded maps, charts, and wires; picking clothes others call mismatched; and not seeing the number in a field of colored dots that friends read instantly. Blue-yellow confusion (rare) shows up as blues looking green and yellows washing out.

How to check — and what a result means

The fastest self-check is an Ishihara-style plate test: a number hides in colored dots along a confusion line, visible to normal color vision and invisible to a matching deficiency. If you miss several plates that others read easily, that’s a genuine signal.

A screen test is a screen, not a diagnosis — displays aren’t color-calibrated. But it’s a solid first step. If it flags a likely deficiency, or if your color vision seems to have changed recently, see an optometrist, who can confirm the type and severity with calibrated plates or an anomaloscope.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if I am colorblind?
Look for consistent color confusions — reds with greens and browns, dim traffic-light reds, trouble with color-coded charts — then take an Ishihara-style plate test. Missing plates others read easily is a strong sign; an eye doctor confirms it.
Do colorblind people see in black and white?
Almost never. That’s total color blindness (achromatopsia), which is extremely rare. The vast majority of colorblind people see plenty of color but confuse specific pairs, usually reds and greens.
Can you suddenly become colorblind?
Inherited color blindness is present from birth. A sudden change in color vision is different — it can follow eye disease, some medications, or optic-nerve problems — and should be checked by an eye-care professional promptly.
